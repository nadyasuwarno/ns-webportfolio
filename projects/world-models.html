01Overview

A hands-on learning station at the Museum of the Moving Image's Emerging Technology Lab. It shows anyone, including people who've never touched AI, how a single sentence becomes a walkable 3D world. Shipped in 2026.

02Context

From March to July 2026, I joined the lab, whose philosophy is "back to fundamentals." My visitors were the public: families, students, curious walk-ins. The technology was anything but fundamental: seeds, noise functions, language models writing code, 3D meshes.

03The problem

People don't fear AI because it's hard. They fear it because they can't see inside it.

A wall of definitions would only confirm that.

How might we open the black box without turning a museum visit into a lecture?

04What I learned

I built a three-question lens, then ran every system through it.

What does it do? Why was it built? How was it built? I graded each paper's claims as strong, moderate or weak, and mapped Meta's WorldGen pipeline stage by stage. Three things changed my approach:

01. Visitors have already played this. Flappy Bird, Minecraft and Pokémon all generate their worlds with code.

02. A few simple ideas carry the whole system: a seed is the world's DNA, noise makes it feel natural, and if/then rules are its laws of physics.

03. The AI pipeline has four clear jobs: plan, check, separate, refine. And jobs can be played.

05What I made

Every visitor gets a role.

The station starts with games they already know, then turns each stage of the pipeline into game stages:

Designer — drag words from a messy diary into the plan, and a wireframe world appears.

Safety inspector — paint the floor you can walk on. Walls push back.

Organizer — split a fused village into 10 separate objects.

Artist — brush blurry shapes into detail, then step inside your world in VR.

We decided to proceed with Lingbot, an open-source world model, over Google's better-known Genie 3 for its openness, even though it meant running a 160GB model.
